# Rules from *The Culture Map*, as a skill for leaders of teams across cultures

Fifty rules from Erin Meyer's *The Culture Map* (2014): the ideas that still hold, or hold harder, now that AI tools
draft, translate and summarise much of what colleagues say across cultures and agents take part in the work. Nothing
in the book breaks; the ideas that bend are given in the form in which they still hold.

Free. One folder in the open Agent Skills format: `working-across-cultures/SKILL.md` plus four reference files. Copy
the folder into your agent's skills directory.

- Why each rule got its ruling: [the verdict](https://beneaththeclaim.com/writing/the-culture-map-in-the-age-of-ai/)
- The book in its own world: [the review](https://beneaththeclaim.com/writing/the-culture-map-in-its-own-time/)

The skill is not affiliated with, sponsored by or endorsed by the book's author or publisher, and quotes none of the book.

## Tested

On three tasks written by someone who had not seen the skill, answers made **4.3 of 5 expected moves with it and
3.7 without**. Three fresh runs per task and arm, answers filed under random ids, two blind graders who agreed on
118 of 120 scores, and six hidden control answers that scored 5 and 0 as they should.

What that means and what it does not:

- The bare model already makes most of these moves: it keeps cross-cultural training when translation arrives,
  rebuilds a case in the order the audience expects, and reads a polished, AI-drafted message stream as part of a
  problem rather than proof that all is well.
- The gains are the readings a tool in between calls for: asking whether a translation made a team sound vague,
  having the reader adjust rather than only the colleague, telling the model what the audience expects, finding out
  how this particular audience likes a case built, and agreeing in writing how fast each side raises a problem.
- No move scored lower with the skill.
- Two moves no answer made, with or without it: telling a translation tool which register to use for a reader, and
  warning that a post-mortem drafted by an assistant could read as insincere.
- The test shows that answers make the moves. It does not show that the advice would work.
- Author, answering model and graders were the same model family. Three runs a cell, one skill version, one run of
  the test.

The skill was written against what the bare model leaves out on three tasks of my own. It already treated no tone as
culture-free, adjusted feedback for its reader, collected objections before meetings and without names, kept decision
logs and had agents argue against their own proposals. It did not close a meeting with each person stating their next
steps aloud, build trust between sites before it was needed, say which words a tool wrote, ask whether the person or
the tool set a message's tone, explain to a team why its meetings are recorded, label each decision final or open, or
keep instructions to agents plain. Those are the moves in the five rules of `SKILL.md`.
