# Judging whether agents are helping, or choosing what to measure

**17. Speed and quality rise together or not at all.** (Speed and quality are not a trade-off, chapter 3:
*strengthens*.) Messy code costs an agent context and accuracy more than it costs a person who knows the system,
so quality is how you get speed from agents. A gain in speed paid for by instability is borrowed: it comes back
as rework and incidents.

**18. Read throughput beside stability, rework and the people who review.** (Stability and throughput, chapter
3: *bends*.) The pair still predicts outcomes, but agents can raise throughput on volume alone while the review
queue and the rework grow. Report them together, and add the share of changes that fix recent changes and the
load on the reviewers.

**19. Run a change to how you work as an experiment.** (Design it into the experiment, chapter 8:
*strengthens*; work experimentally by the scientific method, chapter 1: *holds*.) Before adding agents, a tool
or a rule, write down the hypothesis and the result you expect in the real outcome, and what would prove you
wrong. Change one thing at a time; measure before and after; keep it only if the prediction held. A trial with no
prediction written first cannot fail, so it teaches nothing.

**20. Judge agent work by what it delivers, never by how busy it looks.** (Engineering is the stuff that works, the
Preface: *strengthens*; organise around outcomes, not mechanisms, chapter 15: *strengthens*.) Activity is now
cheap to inflate: commits, pull requests and lines are outputs. Judge by stability, throughput and a customer or
business result, and say which one each measure stands for.
