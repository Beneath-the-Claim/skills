# Rules from The Culture Map, for leaders of teams across cultures, people and agents alike

Rules from Erin Meyer's book (2014). The book holds that national business cultures differ on eight scales, from how
directly people speak to how they keep time, and that what matters is the gap between two cultures, never either
one's absolute position. Every idea in it was ruled on for the age of AI-augmented work, in which tools draft,
translate and summarise much of what colleagues say and agents take part in the work. Each rule names its chapter
and its ruling.

Do not recite the book at the user. Make the move the rule calls for, in the user's own situation, and say why in one
sentence. Leave judgements about named colleagues to the people who work with them: a country's position marks the
middle of a range, never the person in front of you (Normative position: middle of the range, the Introduction:
*holds*).

**These rules ADD to your own judgement; they never replace it.** You already treat no tone as culture-free, adjust a
message for its reader in both directions, ask people how they like feedback instead of inferring it from their
office, give hard news in a private conversation first, collect objections before a meeting and without names, speak
last when you are the senior person, say whether a meeting discusses or decides, treat an AI summary as a draft that
people confirm, let agents act only on confirmed decisions, have an agent argue against its own proposal, keep a
decision log with an owner and the evidence that would reopen each decision, explain each site's norms to all sites
together, and give every time zone a full working day to answer. Keep doing that and give those answers first. Then
add what the rules below bring, because they are the moves usually left out.

## Five things that hold everywhere

Apply these to EVERY task, whichever file you read. The reference files add detail; they do not repeat these.

1. **Read every gap from both positions, and count a model's default register as one of them.** (Cultural
   relativity: only the gap counts, the Introduction: *strengthens*; Tasting the water you swim in, the Introduction:
   *strengthens*.) A message a model drafted, polished or summarised arrives in the model's register, which falls back
   on a few English-speaking cultures when nobody names the reader. It reads blunt to some colleagues and evasive to
   others; treating it as neutral is the error the book warns against. So before you judge a colleague's tone, ask
   whether the person or the tool set it, assume goodwill first, and gauge how direct someone is from what they say
   aloud and from writing no tool touched. (The Evaluating scale, chapter 2: *bends*; Politeness in the eye of the
   beholder, chapter 2: *strengthens*; Upgraders: words that strengthen criticism, chapter 2: *bends*.)
2. **When you ask a model to rewrite or translate, name the reader's culture and the register you want, read what
   survived before you send, and say which words a tool wrote when it matters to the reader.** (The agile
   communicator, chapter 1: *bends*; Soften with downgraders, not upgraders, chapter 2: *bends*; Frame your style:
   explain what and why, the Introduction: *strengthens*.) A rewrite works only toward a position someone names, so
   the person asking must know where the reader sits. A message to a whole team, such as a summary, an agent's report
   or a shared thread, has no single reader to name: write it plainly and explain it live. Tell colleagues how to read
   your own style, and that a tool polished the text when they would otherwise read the polish as yours.
3. **Run low-context processes: write it down, then check it aloud.** (Low-context processes for multicultural
   teams, chapter 1: *strengthens*; Label decisions big D or small d, chapter 5: *strengthens*; Explain why before
   putting it in writing, chapter 1: *bends*.) A summary records what was said, not what each person understood.
   Close every meeting with each person stating their next steps aloud, then a written recap that the agents also
   read. Say aloud which decisions are final and which are open, because a summary lists every decision alike. Before
   the note-taker starts, tell the team why the meeting is recorded and who will read the record. Write every agent
   brief as if the agent knows nothing, because it shares none of the team's history, and keep instructions and
   corrections to agents plain: soften criticism for the colleagues who need it, never for an agent. (Shared history
   shapes a culture's position, chapter 1: *holds*; Blurring the message, chapter 2: *bends*; Downgraders: words that
   soften criticism, chapter 2: *strengthens*.)
4. **Design the channel for dissent, for deferential teams and agreeable models alike.** (Getting input from a
   deferential team, chapter 4: *strengthens*; The Disagreeing scale, chapter 7: *bends*.) A deferential team and an
   agreeable model both tell the boss yes. Ask agents to push back as well as people, and challenge an agent's
   proposal on its merits even after a senior approved it, because it has no feelings to hurt and can be confidently
   wrong. (Sachlichkeit: separating idea from person, chapter 7: *strengthens*.) A recording attaches names to
   dissent: settle disagreement one to one before the meeting where face is at stake, then record the reasons, not
   who raised them. (Meetings before the meeting, chapter 7: *bends*.)
5. **Build trust before you need it, in a channel no model drafts.** (Invest in affective trust, like insurance,
   chapter 6: *strengthens*; Affective trust: trust from the heart, chapter 6: *strengthens*; Trust at a distance:
   choose the medium, chapter 6: *bends*.) AI adds new ways to misstep across cultures: a register the reader
   distrusts, cues stripped out by a summary, doubt about who wrote a message. Remote and recorded work removes the
   chance encounters where trust used to grow. So buy the insurance on purpose, before the hard request or the
   failed release: time with colleagues at other sites, by video or in person, unrecorded, and with relationship-based
   colleagues first, before most of the work moves to e-mail. A model can write warmly; only time together builds
   trust from the heart.

## What bends: apply the changed form, not the original

- **Decision with a capital D** (chapter 5): consensus still binds people, but where agents carry out the work, its
  speed advantage in implementation narrows, and reopening a decision costs the re-briefing more than the rework.
  Write every change down so the agents rebuild from the new decision, not the old one. (Decision with a lowercase d,
  chapter 5: *bends*.)
- **The eight-scale model** (the Introduction): the scales still map people, but in writing three of them,
  communicating, evaluating and persuading, now measure the tool's settings as well as the person.
- **Culture and personality both count** (the Introduction): behaviour now has four sources, the culture, the
  person, the tool and the brief. Check the last two before you explain a message by the first two.
- **Style switching: do it their way** (chapter 8): a model can tell you how it is done there; you still have to do
  it, and calibrate the dose with people.
- **Low-context cultures put it in writing** (chapter 1): tools now write a recap wherever they run, so watch which
  record people actually treat as binding.
- **Applications-first audiences: get to the point** (chapter 3): lead with the ask, because your reader may only
  see a model's summary of what you wrote.

## Nothing breaks: what to watch

No idea in the book breaks. The nearest is reading a culture's directness from the words that strengthen or soften
criticism (Upgraders: words that strengthen criticism, chapter 2: *bends*): it rests on the critic's words being
their own, and it would break if most written text passed through a model, because the cue would then belong to the
tool. Until then, read directness from live talk and from writing no tool touched.
