# Meetings, decisions and disagreement in a mixed team of people and agents

**16. Agree what counts as decided before agents act on the meeting notes.** (Agree the decision-making method
upfront, chapter 5: *strengthens*; Both systems work under shared rules, chapter 5: *strengthens*.) Consensual and
top-down styles both work when everyone knows the rule. Agents act on whatever the record says, so the rule must be
settled before the record exists.

**17. Get in before the meeting, because the summary shows the decision and not where it was made.** (Nemawashi:
root-binding before the meeting, chapter 5: *strengthens*.) In consensual cultures the real decision forms in
one-to-one talks beforehand. A meeting summary records only the formal moment.

**18. Name your tools' default way of deciding, along with your own.** (Unaware of your own decision system, chapter 5:
*strengthens*.) A tool that drafts the plan or counts the votes carries a decision style too; say what it is, so
nobody mistakes it for the team's.

**19. With a consensual team, let the tools carry the correspondence and spend your own effort on the informal
talks; with a top-down team, use them to gather input fast, then decide.** (Working with a more consensual team,
chapter 5: *bends*; Working with a more top-down team, chapter 5: *holds*.) Either team still expects the decision to
be made its way.

**20. Say what each meeting is for, aloud and on the record.** (Frame what your meetings are for, chapter 7: *bends*.)
Otherwise the summary decides for you whether it was a debate, a briefing or a decision.

**21. Frame your disagreement in words the summary keeps, or let a model argue the other side.** (Frame your
disagreeing: devil's advocate, chapter 7: *bends*; Thesis, antithesis, synthesis: meetings as debate, chapter 7:
*bends*.) Where open debate is natural, debate still finds hidden flaws. Where it costs face, a model playing devil's
advocate lets the flaw be said without anyone saying it.

**22. Judge disagreement by what it does to the relationship, not by the volume.** (Speaking with passion is not
disagreeing, chapter 7: *holds*; Four quadrants: disagreement against expressiveness, chapter 7: *bends*.) Emotion and
disagreement are separate dials. Place colleagues on both from live talk: written and summarised text flattens the
emotional one.

**23. Detach ideas from names where dissent costs face.** (Depersonalise disagreement, chapter 7: *strengthens*.)
Recordings and agent logs attach names to every objection. Collect objections anonymously on purpose, and route an
agent's errors to the agent's work, not to the person who directed it.

**24. Invite the quiet colleague, then wait, live and in writing.** (Invite the silent colleague, then wait, the
Introduction: *bends*.) A summary keeps only what was said, so a colleague who waits to be asked is missing from it.

**25. Ask a bicultural colleague what a behaviour meant here.** (Use a cultural bridge, chapter 3: *bends*.) A model
can say what a behaviour usually means; someone who knows both sides can say what it meant in this team.

**26. Watch the fault lines a mixed team forms.** (Fault lines create us versus them, the Epilogue: *holds*; Mix
locations and rotate members, the Epilogue: *bends*.) Translation tools remove the language barrier, not the us
versus them. Mix sites, time zones and channels in the work, and rotate who joins which meeting.

**27. Staff tasks to cultural strengths, and keep a frank human critic.** (Staff tasks to cultural strengths, the
Epilogue: *bends*.) Let a model soften feedback where that helps, but keep at least one person whose plain criticism
the team expects.
