# Writing, rewriting, translating and reading messages, summaries and feedback

**6. Go back to the person for what the summary dropped.** (Communicating scale: low- to high-context, chapter 1:
*strengthens*; Reading the air: listening for meaning, chapter 1: *strengthens*.) High-context meaning lives off the
page, and models and agents only get the page. A summary keeps the words and drops the pauses, the hedges and who
stayed silent. When a summary or a translation decides something that matters, ask the people who were there.

**7. Hear the no said between the lines, before a summary turns it into agreement.** (Hearing a 'no' said between the
lines, chapter 1: *strengthens*.) A polite remark that a request will be hard can be a refusal. Ask open questions
about it in the room, and never let a recap record it as consent.

**8. Say it once, and cut the recaps your drafting tool added.** (Repeating yourself reads as condescending, chapter
1: *strengthens*.) Readers from high-context cultures hear repetition as a judgement of their intelligence, and they
blame the sender, not the tool.

**9. Ask where a translated message started.** (High-context languages, a partial indicator, chapter 1: *bends*;
Small cultural differences can derail work, the Introduction: *strengthens*.) A fluent translation hides the language
and the border it crossed, while the small differences behind it still derail work. Read a translated message with
its sender's culture in mind, not the target language's.

**10. Weigh praise you did not ask for, and give praise that is specific.** (Quadrant C: explicit speech,
praise-wrapped criticism, the Introduction: *strengthens*; Honest, explicit praise before criticism, chapter 2:
*strengthens*.) Machine-written feedback often comes wrapped in praise, so weigh the criticism inside it more heavily.
Generated praise costs nothing and buys little; only specific, honest praise makes the criticism after it heard.

**11. Keep individual criticism private, and praise too where the culture asks it.** (Quadrant D: individual feedback
in private, chapter 2: *strengthens*.) Recorded meetings and agents that post to shared channels make feedback public
by default. Turn the recorder off, or move to a one-to-one, before you criticise one person.

**12. Do not imitate a blunter culture's directness, in person or through a model's rewrite.** (Do not copy a more
direct culture, chapter 2: *holds*.) Asking a tool to "make it as direct as they are" puts you outside the licence
the locals have; explain your own style instead.

**13. Do not read how directly a culture criticises from how directly it talks.** (Do not confuse Communicating with
Evaluating, chapter 2: *holds*.) A colleague can leave much unsaid and still criticise bluntly, or the reverse; a
polished draft can hide both.

**14. Watch which record people treat as binding.** (Low-context cultures put it in writing, chapter 1: *bends*.)
Where the tool writes a recap for everyone, some colleagues treat it as the agreement and others as a draft. Say which
it is.

**15. Coach people to suspend judgement on a tone a tool may have written.** (Coach cultural flexibility as a bridge,
the Epilogue: *bends*.) A colleague who reacts to a polished e-mail's warmth, or its chill, is reacting to the tool as
much as to the sender.
